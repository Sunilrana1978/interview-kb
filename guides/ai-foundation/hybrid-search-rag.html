Hybrid Search Inside RAG,
and How to Build It on Google AI

A deep-dive reference on why retrieval-augmented generation needs both dense and sparse retrieval, how the two are fused into one ranked list, and three concrete ways to implement it on Vertex AI — from a fully managed option to a do-it-yourself pipeline you control end to end.

01 What hybrid search actually is

A RAG system answers a question by first retrieving the passages most likely to contain the answer, then handing them to an LLM to generate a grounded response. Retrieval quality is the ceiling on the whole system — the model can't reason its way past a chunk that was never fetched. Hybrid search is a retrieval strategy that runs two different, complementary search algorithms over the same corpus and merges their results into one ranked list instead of relying on a single method.

The two algorithms are almost always:

  • DENSE  Semantic vector search — embed the query and every chunk into the same high-dimensional space, retrieve by cosine or dot-product similarity.
  • SPARSE  Lexical / keyword search — classic inverted-index term matching, scored with something like BM25, or Google's native full-text search.

Their outputs are combined with a FUSION step — a scoring formula that produces one ranking from two independently-ordered lists — before the top-k chunks are passed to the generator.

02 Why dense retrieval alone falls short

Embedding models are excellent at capturing meaning and paraphrase, which is exactly what makes them blind to a specific, common failure mode: they smooth over the exact tokens that sometimes matter most.

Query patternDense aloneSparse alone
Product/part/model numbers, SKUs, error codes ("ORA-01555")Often misses — near-random tokens don't embed distinctivelyExact match, very strong
Acronyms, ticket IDs, proper nouns unseen in trainingWeak — out-of-distribution tokens compress poorlyStrong if the term appears verbatim
Paraphrased or conceptual questions ("how do I get my money back")Strong — captures intent, not wordingWeak — misses if "refund" never appears
Negation, numeric thresholds, precise constraintsWeak — negation barely shifts embeddingsNeutral to weak
Cross-lingual or synonym-heavy queriesStrongWeak

Neither method dominates the other — they fail on different, largely non-overlapping query types. That non-overlap is precisely what makes combining them worthwhile: hybrid search's recall gain comes from the union of what each method independently gets right, not from either one getting individually better.

Rule of thumb

If your corpus contains identifiers, codes, names, units, or domain jargon that a user might paste in verbatim (support tickets, legal clauses, part catalogs, log lines), you need the sparse lane. If your users ask natural-language questions that rarely reuse the source document's exact wording, you need the dense lane. Real enterprise corpora — PLM data, ServiceNow tickets, Confluence docs — usually need both at once.

03 Anatomy of a hybrid pipeline

Every hybrid implementation, however it's built, follows the same five stages:

1 · Ingest & chunk

Split source documents into chunks (typically 200–800 tokens, often with overlap). The same chunks feed both lanes — you are not maintaining two separate corpora, just two indexes over one corpus.

2 · Dual-index

DENSE Embed each chunk with a text-embedding model and write it to a vector index (ANN — approximate nearest neighbor). SPARSE Tokenize each chunk and write it to an inverted index / full-text index, typically scored with BM25 or a similar term-frequency function.

3 · Dual-retrieve

At query time, run both searches independently against the same query text, each returning its own top-N candidates with its own scores. These are on incompatible scales — cosine similarity ∈ [-1,1] vs. an unbounded BM25 score — which is why they can't just be added together.

4 · Fuse

FUSION Merge the two ranked lists into one, using either Reciprocal Rank Fusion or a normalized weighted sum (see next section).

5 · (Optional) Rerank

Pass the fused top-k through a cross-encoder or a dedicated reranking API for a final, more expensive but more accurate reordering before the chunks reach the LLM's context window.

04 Fusion math: how two rankings become one

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) — the default choice

RRF ignores the raw scores entirely and works purely off rank position, which sidesteps the scale-mismatch problem completely. For a document d that appears at rank r in a result list, its RRF contribution from that list is:

score(d)  =  Σlists   1 / (k + ranklist(d))

Summed across the DENSE and SPARSE lists, with k a smoothing constant (60 is the near-universal default from the original paper). A document that ranks highly in either list gets a strong overall score; a document appearing in both gets a boost from both terms. RRF needs no tuning, no score normalization, and is the built-in default in Vertex AI Search and most hybrid systems for exactly that reason.

Weighted / convex combination — the tunable alternative

If you want explicit control over how much each lane matters, normalize both score lists to [0,1] (min-max is common) and combine linearly:

score(d)  =  α · dense_score(d)  +  (1-α) · sparse_score(d)

where α ∈ [0,1] is your dense/sparse weight — this is the parameter you'll see literally named alpha in Vertex AI RAG Engine's hybrid config. α = 1 is pure dense, α = 0 is pure sparse; most enterprise corpora land somewhere around 0.5–0.7 and are tuned empirically (see §10).

Which to pick

Use RRF when you want a robust default with zero tuning. Use weighted/alpha fusion when you have evaluation data and want to explicitly dial the mix per use case — e.g. a support-ticket corpus full of error codes might warrant α = 0.3 (favor sparse), while a policy-document Q&A corpus might warrant α = 0.8 (favor dense).

06 Build A — Vertex AI Search (managed hybrid, fastest path)

Vertex AI Search runs dense and keyword retrieval together automatically — there is no fusion step for you to configure, which makes this the right starting point if you just need hybrid retrieval to work.

Enable the API & create a Search app

In the Google Cloud console, go to Agent Builder → Search, enable the Discovery Engine API, and create a new App of type "Search," industry "Generic."

Create a Data Store and import documents

Create a Data Store pointing at Cloud Storage (PDF/HTML/TXT/DOCX), BigQuery, a website, or a third-party connector. Vertex AI Search handles chunking, embedding, and indexing internally — you don't manage either lane by hand.

Query with hybrid search active by default

Call the Search endpoint — hybrid dense+keyword fusion runs automatically, no flag required.

# REST — search a Vertex AI Search app
POST https://discoveryengine.googleapis.com/v1/projects/PROJECT_ID/locations/global/collections/default_collection/engines/ENGINE_ID/servingConfigs/default_search:search

{
  "query": "warranty terms for part number XJ-4471",
  "pageSize": 10,
  "queryExpansionSpec": { "condition": "AUTO" },
  "spellCorrectionSpec": { "mode": "AUTO" }
}

Wire it into a RAG answer with Vertex AI Search's grounded generation

Use the :answer endpoint (or the Gemini API's Vertex AI Search grounding tool) to get retrieval + citation-grounded generation in one call, or take the raw ranked chunks and hand them to Gemini yourself for full prompt control.

Trade-off

You don't get to see or tune the fusion formula. If your evaluation shows the built-in mix under- or over-weights keyword matches for your corpus, move to Option B or C.

07 Build B — Vertex AI RAG Engine with explicit alpha

RAG Engine (part of the Vertex AI SDK) gives you a managed corpus and retrieval index, but exposes the alpha weight from the weighted-fusion formula in §04 directly in the retrieval config — the middle ground between "fully managed" and "fully custom."

Create a RAG Corpus

from vertexai import rag
import vertexai

vertexai.init(project="your-project", location="us-central1")

corpus = rag.create_corpus(
    display_name="plm-support-corpus",
    embedding_model_config=rag.EmbeddingModelConfig(
        publisher_model="publishers/google/models/text-embedding-005"
    ),
)

Import documents (chunking handled for you)

rag.import_files(
    corpus_name=corpus.name,
    paths=["gs://your-bucket/plm-docs/"],
    chunk_size=512,
    chunk_overlap=100,
)

Query with hybrid search + explicit alpha

This is the key call — vector_distance_threshold and the retrieval config expose the dense/sparse mix directly:

response = rag.retrieval_query(
    rag_resources=[rag.RagResource(rag_corpus=corpus.name)],
    text="What is the RMA process for part XJ-4471?",
    rag_retrieval_config=rag.RagRetrievalConfig(
        top_k=10,
        hybrid_search=rag.HybridSearch(alpha=0.5),  # 0=sparse only, 1=dense only
        ranking=rag.Ranking(
            rank_service=rag.RankService(model_name="semantic-ranker-512@latest")
        ),
    ),
)
for ctx in response.contexts.contexts:
    print(ctx.source_uri, ctx.score, ctx.text[:120])

Ground a Gemini generation call with the retrieved contexts

from vertexai.generative_models import GenerativeModel, Tool

model = GenerativeModel(
    "gemini-2.5-pro",
    tools=[Tool.from_retrieval(
        retrieval=rag.Retrieval(
            source=rag.VertexRagStore(rag_resources=[rag.RagResource(rag_corpus=corpus.name)])
        )
    )],
)
answer = model.generate_content("What is the RMA process for part XJ-4471?")

08 Ingestion pipeline, end to end

Regardless of which build option you pick, getting documents from wherever they live today into a searchable, hybrid-ready store follows the same shape. For a mixed source set like Cloud Storage files and Confluence pages, here's the concrete path:

1 · Land everything in one place

Cloud Storage is native to Vertex AI Search — point a data store straight at a bucket. Confluence has no first-party direct connector, so the common pattern is exporting or syncing pages out via the Confluence REST API (or a connector tool) into HTML/text files in that same bucket. Cloud Storage becomes the single landing zone even when the origin systems differ.

2 · Choose a chunking strategy

Layout-aware (the managed default) or fixed-size — see §07 for the full comparison and when to pick each.

3 · Attach metadata per chunk

Document title, source URI/path, page number, section heading, last-updated date, and — critically for a mixed corpus — a source-type field (e.g. plm-doc / servicenow-ticket / confluence-page) so you can filter or boost by origin. Layout-aware chunking captures some of this automatically (heading hierarchy, page number, URI); business-specific fields like category or access level you still define explicitly in the data store schema either way.

4 · Ingest

Import runs chunking + embedding (dense) + tokenized indexing (sparse) in one pass — this is the step where, on the managed path, both lanes get built from a single ingestion call.

5 · Citations come for free

Every chunk retains its source URI and metadata, so query results — and grounded-answer responses — carry back the originating document and location automatically.

09 Chunking strategies compared

Layout-aware chunking (the managed default)

Instead of cutting a document every fixed number of tokens, layout-aware chunking reads the document's actual structure first — headings, paragraph boundaries, tables, bullet lists — and cuts along those natural seams. A table stays whole, a paragraph stays whole, and a heading typically stays attached to the paragraph beneath it. That last part matters directly for citation quality: a retrieved chunk that includes its own section heading is self-contained and meaningful, versus a stray paragraph with no context about what section it came from. The trade-off is uneven chunk sizes — some eighty tokens, some six hundred — depending on how the source document happens to be structured.

Fixed-size chunking

You specify chunk size and overlap directly, both measured in the same unit — chunk size is expressed in tokens, there's no separate "token size" setting alongside it. Setting chunk size to 300 means each chunk is roughly 300 tokens; overlap, usually 10–20% of that size, repeats the tail of one chunk into the start of the next so an answer sitting right on a boundary doesn't get split. Beyond those two, the settings that matter are the splitting unit (most implementations default to sentence-aware splitting even within fixed-size, so a cut lands on the nearest sentence boundary rather than mid-word) and the same per-chunk metadata tagging described above — fixed-size chunking doesn't infer any of that for you the way layout-aware partially does.

Pick this when...Layout-awareFixed-size
Source is well-structured (formal docs, Confluence pages with real headings)✓ Default choiceUnnecessary
Source is messy/unstructured (OCR output, raw chat/ticket logs, plain text dumps)Nothing to anchor on✓ Only real option

Tables and structured data inside a document

Small, explanatory tables — a short spec list, a handful of options embedded in a policy doc — are fine to keep inline in the same chunk; the model reads a modest table serialized as text well enough. Large, dense structured data — a big pricing table, a parts catalog, hundreds of rows — is a different situation: cramming it into a text chunk wastes context, and its embedding is close to meaningless since embeddings don't capture tabular relationships well. The better pattern there is to pull it out into a proper structured store, such as a BigQuery table, used for exact lookups or filtering, and leave only a text summary or pointer to it in the document store for retrieval and citation purposes.

10 Parent-child chunking

Parent-child is a separate strategy layered on top of whichever chunking method you use underneath — not a subset of fixed-size, and not natively exposed as a config toggle in Vertex AI Search itself. It keeps two levels of granularity for the same content: small, precise child chunks (100–200 tokens) that are what actually gets embedded and searched, and a larger surrounding parent chunk (the full section, or a few paragraphs) that each child references by ID but that is never itself embedded or searched directly.

Why bother

Small chunks give precise semantic matches; but handing the LLM only that tiny fragment often strips away context it needs to answer well. Parent-child gets both: precise retrieval via the child, coherent complete context via the parent.

How the storage splits

  • Only child chunks are embedded and live in the vector store, each tagged with a parent_id field alongside its vector.
  • Parent chunks are never embedded — they live somewhere simpler and cheaper: a key-value store, a BigQuery table keyed by parent_id, or Cloud Storage with the parent ID as the object path. Since parents are only ever fetched by key, not searched, this can be a plain lookup table.

Query-time flow

query → child match (vector store) → read parent_id → key lookup in parent store → parent text → LLM context

The parent lookup is a direct key fetch, not another search, so it's typically single-digit to low-tens of milliseconds — and firing lookups for your top few child matches in parallel keeps this from adding meaningfully to end-to-end latency. Vertex AI Search and RAG Engine don't offer this as a built-in toggle; you implement the two-store pattern yourself on top of either. Frameworks like LangChain and LlamaIndex do have this built in as a "parent document retriever" abstraction if you'd rather not hand-roll it (see §10).

11 How managed hybrid search works internally

With Vertex AI Search, there's nothing to turn on — hybrid retrieval is active by default, and you never provision or manage a second index yourself. From a single ingestion pass, the service builds and stores two representations of every chunk against one logical store: a dense embedding vector for semantic matching, and a tokenized, indexed representation of the same text for keyword matching.

At query time, you send one plain natural-language query — no special syntax, no quoting for exact-match mode, no prefix to request semantic vs. keyword. Internally the service runs that same query text against both indexes simultaneously, producing a semantic ranking and a keyword ranking, then fuses them with reciprocal rank fusion before returning a single result list.

What you actually get back

One already-fused, already-ranked, already-cited list. You don't see the two intermediate rankings, you don't run reciprocal rank fusion yourself, and there's no massaging step on your end — that's the entire value proposition of the managed path. Inspecting the two lanes separately for debugging is only possible on the DIY path (§08), where you call Vector Search and BigQuery independently and get two literal result sets before fusing them yourself.

Vertex AI RAG Engine behaves the same way under the hood, with one difference: it exposes the alpha weight from §04 directly, so you can tilt the dense/sparse balance rather than accepting the default fusion as-is.

12 Build C — fully custom: Vector Search + BigQuery + your own RRF

For maximum control — custom fusion logic, per-tenant filtering, or corpora too large/latency-sensitive for the managed options — run the two lanes yourself: DENSE Vertex AI Vector Search (Matching Engine) for the ANN index, SPARSE BigQuery's native SEARCH() full-text function (or Cloud SQL/AlloyDB full-text search) for the keyword index, fused with RRF in application code.

1 · Index the dense lane — Vertex AI Vector Search

from vertexai.language_models import TextEmbeddingModel
from google.cloud import aiplatform

embed_model = TextEmbeddingModel.from_pretrained("text-embedding-005")
vectors = [e.values for e in embed_model.get_embeddings(chunks)]

# upsert into a deployed Vector Search index (datapoints keyed by chunk_id)
index.upsert_datapoints(datapoints=[
    {"datapoint_id": cid, "feature_vector": vec}
    for cid, vec in zip(chunk_ids, vectors)
])

2 · Index the sparse lane — BigQuery search index

CREATE SEARCH INDEX chunk_text_idx
ON plm_support.chunks(chunk_text);

-- sparse retrieval at query time, scored by BQ's native relevance function
SELECT chunk_id, chunk_text,
       SEARCH_SCORE(chunk_text, @query_text) AS sparse_score
FROM plm_support.chunks
WHERE SEARCH(chunk_text, @query_text)
ORDER BY sparse_score DESC
LIMIT 50;

3 · Query both lanes in parallel

import asyncio

async def hybrid_retrieve(query: str, k: int = 10):
    dense_task  = asyncio.create_task(vector_search_query(query))   # -> [(chunk_id, rank), ...]
    sparse_task = asyncio.create_task(bigquery_search_query(query)) # -> [(chunk_id, rank), ...]
    dense_results, sparse_results = await asyncio.gather(dense_task, sparse_task)
    return reciprocal_rank_fusion(dense_results, sparse_results, k=k)

4 · Fuse with RRF (the formula from §04, in code)

def reciprocal_rank_fusion(*ranked_lists, k: int = 10, rrf_k: int = 60):
    scores = {}
    for ranked_list in ranked_lists:
        for rank, chunk_id in enumerate(ranked_list, start=1):
            scores[chunk_id] = scores.get(chunk_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (rrf_k + rank)
    fused = sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    return fused[:k]

5 · Fetch full chunk text for the fused top-k and pass to Gemini

top_chunk_ids = [cid for cid, _ in fused_results]
context_text = fetch_chunks_by_id(top_chunk_ids)  # your own lookup, e.g. from BigQuery

prompt = f"Answer using only this context:\n{context_text}\n\nQuestion: {query}"
response = GenerativeModel("gemini-2.5-pro").generate_content(prompt)
Why go this far

This path exists for cases like per-tenant ACL filtering fused into scoring, sub-100ms latency budgets, non-Google embedding models, or a fusion formula more advanced than RRF/alpha (e.g. learned fusion weights per query type). If none of that applies, Option A or B will get you there faster and with less to operate.

13 Other frameworks: LangChain, LlamaIndex

Everything above assumes Google-native services end to end. If you'd rather orchestrate retrieval yourself in Python while still using Google embeddings and Gemini for generation, LangChain and LlamaIndex are the two mainstream choices — both work fine against Vertex AI Vector Search or a third-party vector DB, and both have hybrid search and parent-document retrieval as built-in abstractions rather than something you hand-roll.

# LangChain — hybrid via EnsembleRetriever, using Vertex AI embeddings + Gemini
from langchain_google_vertexai import VertexAIEmbeddings, ChatVertexAI
from langchain_community.retrievers import BM25Retriever
from langchain.retrievers import EnsembleRetriever
from langchain_community.vectorstores import FAISS  # or any vector store incl. Vertex AI Vector Search

embeddings = VertexAIEmbeddings(model_name="text-embedding-005")
vector_store = FAISS.from_documents(chunks, embeddings)
dense_retriever = vector_store.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 10})

sparse_retriever = BM25Retriever.from_documents(chunks)
sparse_retriever.k = 10

hybrid_retriever = EnsembleRetriever(
    retrievers=[dense_retriever, sparse_retriever],
    weights=[0.5, 0.5],  # same alpha concept as §04, applied at merge time
)

results = hybrid_retriever.invoke("warranty terms for part XJ-4471")
llm = ChatVertexAI(model="gemini-2.5-pro")
answer = llm.invoke(f"Answer using only this context:\n{results}\n\nQuestion: ...")
Which route to take

If you're already deep in Google Cloud tooling and want the least to operate, stay on Vertex AI Search or RAG Engine (§06–07). Reach for LangChain or LlamaIndex when you want the DIY control of §12 — custom fusion, parent-child, non-Google embeddings — without writing the fusion and retrieval orchestration code yourself; they're a faster way to get to the same place as a hand-rolled Vector Search + BigQuery pipeline.

14 Adding a reranking stage

Fusion (RRF or alpha) is a cheap, first-pass merge across possibly-hundreds of candidates. A FUSION reranker is a second, more expensive model — typically a cross-encoder that scores the (query, chunk) pair jointly rather than comparing independent embeddings — run only on the fused top 20–50 to produce the final top-k. Vertex AI exposes this as the Ranking API (semantic-ranker-512@latest), which you saw wired into Option B's rag_retrieval_config above and can also call standalone against Option C's fused list:

from google.cloud import discoveryengine_v1 as discoveryengine

client = discoveryengine.RankServiceClient()
request = discoveryengine.RankRequest(
    ranking_config="projects/PROJECT_ID/locations/global/rankingConfigs/default_ranking_config",
    model="semantic-ranker-512@latest",
    query=query,
    records=[
        discoveryengine.RankingRecord(id=cid, content=text)
        for cid, text in fused_chunks
    ],
    top_n=6,
)
reranked = client.rank(request=request)

Rule of thumb: fusion decides who gets considered; reranking decides who wins. Skipping fusion and only reranking is expensive (cross-encoders don't scale to the full corpus); skipping reranking and shipping raw fusion output is usually fine to start, and worth adding once you have evaluation data showing it helps.

15 Tuning & evaluating your hybrid mix

  • Build a labeled eval set first. 30–100 realistic queries with the known correct chunk(s) marked, drawn from real support tickets or docs if possible — you cannot responsibly tune alpha without this.
  • Measure Recall@k and MRR for dense-only, sparse-only, RRF, and a sweep of alpha values (0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8). Hybrid should beat both single-lane baselines on recall — if it doesn't, check that your sparse index is actually indexing the right fields.
  • Segment the eval set by query type (identifier lookups vs. conceptual questions) — this is usually where you discover you need per-intent alpha routing rather than one global value.
  • Re-tune chunk size alongside alpha. Chunk boundaries affect BM25 term statistics and embedding quality differently — they aren't independent variables.
  • Watch reranking's marginal gain, not just its absolute score — it's a meaningful latency/cost addition, and on small corpora fusion alone often gets you 90% of the benefit.

16 Pitfalls & best practices

Common mistakes

Adding raw scores without normalizing. Cosine similarity and BM25 live on entirely different scales — a naive sum lets one lane silently dominate. Use RRF, or normalize before weighting.

Retrieving too few candidates per lane before fusing. Fuse from each lane's top 30–50, not top 5 — a chunk that's rank 8 in dense but rank 2 in sparse should still surface, and a too-narrow candidate set kills that.

One global alpha for a mixed-intent corpus. A single weight rarely serves both "find error code E-4471" and "explain the refund policy" well — consider query-type classification with routed alpha values, or just default to RRF.

Skipping the sparse lane's tokenizer/field config. BigQuery SEARCH() and similar functions need the right analyzer settings (stemming, stopwords, case-folding) for your domain vocabulary, or exact-match recall will underperform expectations.

Chunking once and never revisiting it. Chunk size/overlap is often a bigger lever on final quality than the fusion formula itself — treat it as a tunable parameter, not a one-time decision.
